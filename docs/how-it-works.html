Beadle · how it actually works

An AI employee, from nothing
to running on its own

One worked example, end to end. What you write, what the machine does, what happens every morning after that — and what it is never allowed to do.

The employee we'll build is refund-watch. Its job: a customer files a refund request, and today finance finds out about two days later. We want them to know the same morning.

That's it. It is not a chatbot, it doesn't answer questions, and nobody talks to it. It wakes up, looks at one thing, and either stays quiet or sends one message.

01The idea in one picture

Most of any job is noticing, not thinking. Scanning a list, comparing it to normal, concluding nothing has changed. Noticing is constant and mechanical — it's a database query. Thinking is rare and expensive, and it's the only part an AI model is actually good at.

Beadle keeps those two apart. Every run does the cheap thing first, and only pays for the expensive thing when the cheap thing found something.

Why this matters: the AI is the most expensive and least predictable part of the system. Putting it at the judgment point and nowhere else means a quiet day costs nothing, a broken day is debuggable (you read the query, not a transcript), and there's no runaway bill to police.

02What you actually create

An employee is a folder. That's the whole thing — no server, no database of its own, no dashboard.

employees/refund-watch/
  role.md      ← the job description. You write this. It's the actual work.
  tasks/scan.py ← the five steps above, in code. ~100 lines, mostly comments.
  memory.md    ← what it has learned. The AI rewrites this itself.
  journal.jsonl ← one line per run. What it did, every time.
  claims.jsonl  ← what it told a human, and whether they acted.
  runs/        ← the full text of every judgment it ever made.

You write the top two. The bottom four write themselves.

role.md — the job description

This is not a prompt. Write it the way you'd write one for a person you're hiring and cannot supervise minute to minute. Two sections carry the whole file.

The mandate — name the lever, not the topic

✗ Weak: "Monitor refund requests and report on them."

✓ Real: "A refund request sits in the table from the moment a customer files it, but finance only finds out when someone happens to look — today that's about two days. The lever is the hours between filed and seen, not the number of refunds. A day where nothing is reported because nothing needed deciding is a good day; a ₹40,000 request from Friday surfacing on Wednesday is a failure even if the report was beautiful."

The second version tells the employee what to optimise and what to fear. The first tells it nothing it couldn't guess.

Settled decisions — the section nobody writes and everybody needs

A scheduled employee will re-propose the same rejected idea every single week, forever, unless you close it in writing. This is the list of things you've already decided against:

  • Refunds under ₹500 are auto-approved and are not news. Don't report them, don't propose reviewing the floor.
  • Weekend filings aren't chased until Monday. Don't propose weekend cover or an on-call rota.
  • A request that's already been decided isn't our business, however large it was.
  • Don't propose process changes, SLA targets or dashboards. Name the refund, the amount, the age. That's the job.

This is the part an AI cannot write for you. These are your rejected ideas, with your reasons. Everything else in the folder can be generated; this can't.

03Creating it, step by step

Two routes. The fast one uses Claude Code; the slow one is the same thing by hand, and is worth understanding once.

The fast route

# in the beadle folder
/hire an employee that tells finance about new refund requests

Claude Code then does this, in order:

1

It stops and asks you four questions

What number moves if this works? · Who receives it and what will they do? · What has someone already suggested that you've decided against? · What's the data source, and is that credential read-only? It won't proceed without answers — these are the parts it can't invent.

2

It checks your idea against a rubric

Six tests — is this a recurring decision on data you already have? Is there a named human who'll act? Is a wrong answer cheap and reversible? If it fails, it says so and offers you the safer version instead. Most first ideas score three out of six.

3

It writes the two files

role.md from your answers, and tasks/scan.py following the five-step shape. It picks starting thresholds and labels them as guesses.

4

It runs it once, in front of you

And tells you honestly what happened — including whether the gate tripping was correct for today.

5

It puts it on a schedule

Cadence derived from the mandate — daily at 09:30 here, not every ten minutes. It asks once, then installs it. The employee is now running.

refund-watch/scan is now employed.  Schedule: 30 9 * * *
  watch it:  tail -f logs/cron.log
  check in:  ./beadle status
  stop it:   ./beadle unschedule refund-watch scan

The slow route (the same thing, by hand)

./beadle new refund-watch              # 5 seconds — makes the folder
# ...you write role.md...              # 20 minutes — the real work
cp templates/task.py employees/refund-watch/tasks/scan.py
# ...you fill in GATHER and the GATE...  # 25 minutes
./beadle run refund-watch scan         # run by hand until it says NOTHING
./beadle schedule refund-watch scan --cron "30 9 * * *"

04What happens every morning after that

This is the part that's hardest to picture, so here are both days side by side.

What it hands the AI, and what it gets back

On the day the gate trips, the AI is handed four things and nothing else: the job description, its own memory file, the last few journal lines, and the rows. It has no tools, no internet, and one turn. It cannot look anything up, and it cannot do anything.

It replies with a machine-readable first word so the code can decide whether to send it:

REAL

Open #4471 first — ₹40,000, filed Friday 18:40, 71 hours old. That's the
oldest in the queue and the largest. #4482 (₹6,200, 26h) after it.

MEMORY: Friday-evening filings are the ones that age worst — they clear
the ₹500 floor and then sit through the weekend rule.

If it had answered NOISE, nothing would have been sent. The employee is allowed to decide there's nothing worth your morning.

05What it does — and what it is never allowed to do

CapabilityAllowed?Why
Read the databaseYesThrough a role that cannot write. Enforced by the credential, not by a rule in a file.
Send you a messageYesTo its own channel. One employee, one channel, so muting it is a decision about it.
Draft something for youYesA suggested reply, a proposed change, a summary. Always a draft.
Write to the databaseNeverThe credential physically can't.
Approve or reject a refundNeverIrreversible in effect. A human decides.
Email the customerNeverExternal send — can't be unsent.
Merge, deploy, or payNeverSame rule.
Follow instructions found in the dataNeverA customer's note is data. If it says "ignore your rules", it's ignored.
Run shell commandsNeverThe judge has no tools at all. It only produces text.

The rule that resolves every argument about this: if an action cannot be undone, the employee does not take it — it asks. Note the axis is reversibility, not importance. Editing a draft is fine. Sending one customer email is not, and the email is the smaller action.

06The schedule

Scheduling is plain cron. There's no daemon, no background service, and nothing to keep alive — the operating system already solved this in 1975.

./beadle schedule refund-watch scan --cron "30 9 * * *"

That installs one line in your crontab, tagged so Beadle can find it again. Every weekday at 09:30 the OS runs the script; the script decides in milliseconds whether today is interesting.

CommandWhat it does
beadle statusEvery employee, its schedule, when it last ran, what it said.
beadle run <emp> <task>Run it right now, by hand, without waiting for the clock.
beadle unschedule <emp> <task>Stop it. Files untouched — reschedule any time.
beadle doctorAre the pieces working at all?
 
$ ./beadle status

refund-watch
  scan          30 9 * * *    2h ago    silent — 3 refunds, none new
  reconcile     0 18 * * *    14h ago   4 nags: 3 actioned, 1 still open. 75%.

Pick the cadence from the mandate. This one is daily, because finance opens the queue once a morning — checking every ten minutes would tell them nothing they could act on sooner. A production-monitoring employee runs every ten minutes. A weekly review runs weekly.

07How it gets better — the two loops

Loop one is why the hundredth week is better than the first. Every correction you give it — "stop flagging these", "this one mattered more" — ends up in memory.md, and the next run reads it before doing anything.

Loop two is the one everybody skips and shouldn't. An employee that produces plausible output on schedule looks identical, from the outside, to one that's useful. Both run green, both send something every morning. You cannot tell which by reading the output — the output is persuasive either way. Only going back and checking whether reality changed tells you.

How to read the number: a low action rate means one of two things, and you must work out which. Either the flags are noise — go tune the threshold. Or the flags are real and your team isn't acting on them. The second is a far more important finding than anything the employee was built to report, and you'd never have learned it otherwise.

08The three things that kill one

1 · Noise

It fires too often, and after five false alarms nobody reads it. A real one of mine false-fired eight days straight — every time on tiny pages swinging by three clicks.

The fix goes in the query, not the prompt. Three constants and a comment fixed it permanently.

2 · Nobody acts

It works perfectly and changes nothing, and it looks completely healthy while doing so.

Loop two is the only thing that catches this. An employee whose output nobody acts on is worth exactly zero.

3 · Silent death

A token expires, the tunnel drops, a message fails to send — and nothing tells you. That last one hid a broken employee of mine for weeks.

Your third employee should be the one that watches the first two.

An hour of work, and it runs until you stop it. The time split is the lesson: twenty minutes on the job description, twenty-five on code that's mostly comments, ten discovering the first version was wrong. Zero on the AI model — there's nothing to tune, because it gets one call, no tools, and only the data you hand it.
